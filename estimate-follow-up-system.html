How to follow up on estimates: a simple system for service businesses

Most lost revenue in a service business isn't lost in the sale — it's lost after the estimate goes out and no one follows up. Here's the system that fixes it.

Whether you run a landscaping company, an HVAC shop, a cleaning service, or a plumbing business, the pattern is the same: you send an estimate, the customer goes quiet, and everyone gets busy with the next job. Two weeks later, that quote is dead — not because the price was wrong, but because nobody followed up.

Industry data on service businesses consistently shows that a large share of quotes never get a second touch, and that most deals require several follow-ups to close. In other words, the jobs are already won at the estimate stage; they're just never collected. The fix isn't more leads or more hustle. It's a system.

Why follow-up breaks down

Follow-up fails for one reason: it depends on memory. On a busy Tuesday, memory loses. The estimate that needed a nudge is competing with a truck that broke down, a crew that's short-handed, and a phone that won't stop ringing. No one forgets on purpose — the work simply isn't systematized, so it slips.

That's why "we need to follow up more" never sticks as advice. Effort isn't the missing piece. A repeatable process is.

The four parts of a follow-up system

A follow-up system for a service business has four moving parts. Get these right and the leaks close on their own:

  • Every estimate is tracked. The moment a quote goes out, it enters a visible list with a status and an owner — not a memory, not a sticky note.
  • Follow-up runs on a schedule. Approved messages and timing go out automatically (text, email, or a call task) without depending on anyone remembering.
  • Stop and routing rules are automatic. The moment a customer replies, books, or opts out, follow-up stops or routes to the right person — so no one gets pestered and nothing gets dropped.
  • Human attention is flagged. Replies and judgment calls are surfaced to a named person, instead of sitting in a shared inbox everyone assumes someone else is watching.

What "good" looks like

When the system is running, the office can see the whole board: which estimates are new, which have been followed up, which got a reply, and which are aging. You stop guessing and start seeing verified activity — what ran, what came back, and the few things that need a decision. That visibility is the difference between hoping deals close and knowing where every one of them stands.

You don't need more software — you need a system that runs

Most owners already have a CRM or a spreadsheet. The gap isn't a tool; it's that the tool doesn't run itself. The point of a follow-up system is that the right work happens whether or not anyone remembers — and that someone is accountable for the exceptions.
